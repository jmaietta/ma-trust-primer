This primer is tailored to a 75-year-old, unmarried Massachusetts resident who lives alone and owns the home alone. What a trust actually does

A trust is a legal arrangement under which a trustee holds and manages property for beneficiaries, following instructions established by the grantor or settlor—the person creating it. A successor trustee takes over when the original trustee can no longer serve. Principal means the property itself; income generally means earnings such as interest or rent.

The labels describe different things. Revocable or irrevocable describes the ability to change or undo the arrangement. Living or testamentary describes when it is created. Special-needs or charitable describes its purpose. One document may combine several features.

A living trust exists during life. A testamentary trust is established under a will at death. Creating a trust and placing assets into it are separate steps: a signed document alone does not transfer your house.

Sources: SSA: Trust basics; Massachusetts trust creation statute.

How each trust works

Tap a trust name to read its benefits and tradeoffs.

1. Revocable living trust

You typically serve as trustee, retain the benefit of the assets, and can amend or revoke the trust while competent. A successor can manage trust property if you become incapacitated and distribute it after death. Properly funded assets generally avoid probate, although debts, taxes, and trust administration still require attention.

Useful when: you want an orderly transition, more privacy than a probate estate, or continuing management for heirs. It does not itself reduce estate taxes, and Massachusetts law exposes revocable trust property to your creditors.

Sources: IRS trust primer; Massachusetts creditor rules; Massachusetts real-estate trust overview.

2. Irrevocable trust for MassHealth planning

Often called a Medicaid asset protection trust or an income-only trust, this arrangement may keep properly restricted principal from being counted for long-term-care eligibility. The document must prevent principal from being available to you under the applicable rules; income payable to you may still count. Merely naming it “irrevocable” does not establish protection.

Funding it can trigger the five-year transfer rules. It may reserve carefully drafted occupancy rights and permit a trustee to sell and buy replacement property. Sale proceeds generally remain subject to the trust’s restrictions.

At 75: first establish how you would pay for care without accessing transferred principal. Do not assume a child can return trust money whenever you need it.

Source: 130 CMR 520.023: MassHealth trust rules. Occupancy and trustee powers require case-specific drafting and review.

3. Testamentary trust

Your will directs that an inheritance be held in trust after death. It can manage funds for a grandchild or a relative who needs assistance with money. It offers no lifetime incapacity management, and property going through your will must ordinarily pass through probate before funding the trust.

Useful when: the principal objective is controlling an inheritance after death. A living trust can also create continuing trusts for heirs at death.

Source: Massachusetts methods of creating a trust.

4. Special-needs or supplemental-needs trust

A third-party trust can hold your money for a child or other beneficiary with disabilities, with distributions designed to supplement public benefits. Properly structured, it generally avoids the Medicaid repayment requirement applicable to many trusts funded with the beneficiary’s own assets.

A first-party special-needs trust uses the disabled beneficiary’s own money. The usual federal exception requires creation and funding before age 65. It is therefore not a routine new solution for a 75-year-old’s own assets. Nonprofit pooled trusts have separate rules; at 75, do not assume funding one is free of MassHealth transfer penalties.

Sources: SSA: special-needs and pooled-trust exceptions; Third-party trust distinctions; MassHealth transfer rules.

5. Spendthrift or discretionary trust for heirs

Instead of an outright inheritance, a trustee controls payments under standards you establish. This can help an heir who struggles with money, addiction, or creditor exposure. A spendthrift clause restricts assignment and creditor access before distribution, subject to legal exceptions.

This protects an inheritance for someone else; it does not automatically protect property you put into a trust for your own benefit. Money actually distributed may lose trust protection.

Sources: Massachusetts spendthrift provisions; Claims against a trust’s creator.

6. Massachusetts nominee realty trust

This title-holding arrangement commonly lets beneficiaries direct the trustee. Beneficial interests are recorded on a separate schedule, which should be preserved with the estate documents.

It may help with privacy and multiple owners, but the label alone does not avoid probate, reduce taxes, or protect against MassHealth. An individually owned beneficial interest may still require probate. Often another estate-planning trust holds that interest.

Sources: Margolis Bloom: nominee trusts and beneficiary schedules; Rudolph Friedmann: probate implications.

7. Other specialized trusts
  • Irrevocable life-insurance trust (ILIT): owns insurance and controls proceeds for beneficiaries; with proper structure, proceeds can be outside the insured’s taxable estate. Transferring an existing policy raises a federal three-year estate-inclusion rule. At 75, existing coverage, premiums, and insurability are practical constraints. IRS estate-return instructions.
  • Qualified personal residence trust (QPRT): transfers a home while retaining occupancy for a fixed term. The intended estate-tax benefit generally requires surviving that term; afterward, continued occupancy typically requires fair-market rent. Mortality risk and loss of flexibility deserve close attention at 75. Schwab: QPRTs.
  • Charitable remainder trust: makes prescribed payments to individuals, with the remainder going to charity. It can provide a partial charitable deduction and defer recognition of gains through distributions, but payments are not automatically tax-free. A charitable lead trust reverses the order: charity benefits first, then family. These fit substantial charitable goals. IRS: charitable remainder trusts.
  • Irrevocable burial trust: reserves funds for qualifying funeral and burial expenses and may be noncountable under MassHealth rules. It is a limited-purpose arrangement, not a vehicle for protecting the house or a general investment portfolio. MassHealth noncountable-asset rules.

What changes when your home goes into a trust?

The deed, mortgage, insurance, homestead filing, and any property-tax relief must work with the trust. Ask who can authorize a sale, who pays repairs and taxes, whether you can refinance, and what happens if you move permanently into care.

Massachusetts home-counting issue: MassHealth regulations expressly treat a nursing-facility resident’s home in a revocable trust as countable. A transfer into a routine living trust therefore deserves specific elder-law review before it happens. 130 CMR 520.023(B)(4).

Homestead is separate. Massachusetts provides $125,000 of automatic protection and up to $1 million through a declared homestead, subject to exceptions. At 75, an eligible owner can qualify for up to $1 million of individual elderly homestead protection. As the sole owner, this homeowner has no second owner’s homestead protection to aggregate. Trust beneficiaries may qualify, with the trustee making the appropriate declaration.

Homestead protection does not erase mortgages or tax liens and is not a substitute for MassHealth planning. Ask the attorney to preserve the correct protection when recording a trust deed.

Source: Massachusetts Secretary: Homestead FAQs.

A direct deed to children or a retained life estate is another possible arrangement, but neither is a trust. Compare the effect on sale flexibility, children’s creditors, taxes, and MassHealth before choosing one.

Keep three tax questions separate

Estate tax: Massachusetts has a $2 million estate-tax threshold, implemented through statutory rules including a credit of up to $99,600. The federal basic estate-and-gift exclusion is $15 million per person in 2026, before accounting for prior taxable gifts. A family can face Massachusetts tax while owing no federal estate tax.

Count more than bank accounts: the home, investments, retirement accounts, and includible life-insurance proceeds can matter. A house worth $1.2 million plus $1 million of other assets warrants an estate-tax review; that is a screening example, not a tax calculation.

Sources: Massachusetts estate-tax law; IRS: 2026 exclusion.

Capital-gains tax: inherited property generally receives a basis tied to its value at death. Lifetime gifts generally carry the giver’s basis for calculating gain. For a greatly appreciated home, that difference can materially affect heirs’ later taxes.

An irrevocable trust’s income-tax status alone does not guarantee a basis adjustment. IRS Revenue Ruling 2023-2 denies the adjustment in the described case where trust property is outside the grantor’s gross estate. Some MassHealth trusts deliberately preserve estate inclusion to support a basis adjustment. Ask the attorney to explain the result for your exact document.

Sources: IRS: gifts and inheritances; IRS Revenue Ruling 2023-2.

Annual income tax: a grantor trust generally reports income through its owner; a nongrantor trust can have separate returns and beneficiary reporting. “Irrevocable” does not necessarily mean a separate income-tax taxpayer. Ask who pays tax on income, a home sale, and retained gains. IRS trust primer.

What happens at death?

Revocable trust: the deceased grantor’s provisions generally become irrevocable, and the successor trustee takes over. The trustee may distribute property or retain it in continuing trusts.

Irrevocable trust: the document determines whether it continues or distributes. The grantor’s death does not automatically terminate it, and “irrevocable” does not establish that its assets are outside the taxable estate.

Testamentary trust: the personal representative administers the will and funds the trust through the estate. The trustee then manages the inheritance. A trustee and a personal representative have different jobs, even if the same person serves in both roles.

Sources: IRS trust primer; Massachusetts trust creation statute.

The taxes your family should distinguish

QuestionPractical answer
Is there an inheritance tax?Massachusetts currently imposes an estate tax, rather than a separate tax on an heir simply receiving an inheritance. Another state’s rules may matter if property or beneficiaries have ties there.
Does avoiding probate avoid estate tax?No. Taxable estates can include trust assets, jointly held assets, insurance, and beneficiary-designated accounts.
Are inherited dollars income?An inheritance of principal generally is not income. Income earned afterward and taxable retirement-account distributions are different.
Does putting a home in trust erase capital gains?No. The basis at death, estate inclusion, sale price, and available exclusions determine the result.

Sources: Massachusetts estate and inheritance-tax distinction; IRS Publication 559: estates, beneficiaries, and income in respect of a decedent.

Estate-tax planning for an unmarried owner

Plan around the owner’s individual Massachusetts estate-tax position. There is no current spouse to receive property using a marital deduction, and creating several trusts does not multiply the owner’s estate-tax exemption. A revocable trust helps administer the estate but generally leaves its assets in the taxable estate.

The comparison to request is: retain assets until death, make lifetime gifts, or use an appropriately designed irrevocable trust. Model estate tax, heirs’ eventual capital-gains tax, and the owner’s remaining access to funds together. An irrevocable MassHealth trust may preserve estate inclusion and a basis adjustment without reducing estate tax.

If previously widowed: ask the adviser to review any federal unused exclusion preserved from a deceased spouse and any existing bypass or QTIP trust. These may still affect the current estate. They are not new spousal-planning strategies for an unmarried person. No prior marriage is assumed here.

Sources: IRS estate-tax FAQs; IRS basis ruling; Massachusetts estate-tax law.

Make the home’s inheritance instructions specific

Name primary and alternate beneficiaries rather than assuming which relatives will inherit. If an unmarried partner, friend, or caregiver should benefit, identify that person expressly in the appropriate documents. Coordinate the trust, will, deed, and account beneficiary designations.

Decide whether the trustee should sell the home and divide proceeds, transfer it to one beneficiary, or allow someone to live there for a defined period. For shared or continuing ownership, specify who pays taxes, insurance, and repairs; when a sale can occur; and how a beneficiary can buy out the others. Ask how estate taxes and administration expenses will be allocated between beneficiaries and assets.

Administration and deadlines

Arrange date-of-death valuations, secure the property, identify beneficiaries, and determine debts and taxes before distributing everything. Keep a cash reserve: an estate can owe tax while most of its value is tied up in a house.

A Massachusetts M-706 filing requirement and the amount of tax owed are separate questions; gross estate and adjusted taxable gifts can affect filing. A required Massachusetts return and payment are generally due nine months after death. Federal Form 706 also generally has a nine-month deadline. Ask promptly about filing extensions and payment rules. If previously widowed, also review any existing federal portability election.

The deceased person’s final income-tax return is separate from estate-tax returns. An estate or continuing trust may also need income-tax returns for income earned after death. Traditional retirement-account income generally does not receive the home-style basis adjustment; beneficiary and distribution rules need separate review.

Sources: Massachusetts DOR: filing deadline; DOR: filing and taxable gifts; IRS estate-tax FAQs; IRS: final income-tax return; IRS Publication 559.

Long-term care: the timing problem

MassHealth long-term-care transfer rules generally examine the preceding 60 months. Signing a trust does not start the clock for property added later. A disqualifying transfer can cause a benefit penalty that begins when the person is otherwise eligible for long-term-care coverage, rather than simply running from the gift date.

For illustration, property transferred at 75 may be outside that look-back around age 80. That is not a promise of eligibility: other assets, income, care needs, trust terms, and later transfers still matter.

With no current spouse, spousal transfer exceptions are not available for this plan. If care is needed soon, investigate other permissible transfers before funding a new trust. Certain transfers to a qualifying disabled child or caregiver child can receive different treatment. Because the homeowner currently lives alone, do not assume the caregiver-child exception applies. Precise facts and documentation control.

Source: 130 CMR 520.019: transfer timing, penalties, and exceptions.

Eligibility and estate recovery are different. Eligibility concerns whether MassHealth pays for care now; estate recovery concerns repayment after death. For deaths on or after August 1, 2024, Massachusetts narrowed recovery to federally required categories. These include specified long-term-care services received at age 55 or older and certain institutional care. The statute defines the recoverable estate as the probate estate, provides protections involving certain surviving children, and separately addresses liens. For this unmarried owner, there is no surviving-spouse delay to rely on.

Avoiding probate can therefore matter, but does not establish eligibility or remove an existing lien. An exempt home during life is not necessarily immune from later recovery.

Source: Massachusetts estate-recovery statute.

How to approach the decision

If the primary concern is incapacity and orderly inheritance, discuss a funded revocable trust while reviewing the home’s MassHealth treatment. If it is future care costs, compare an irrevocable plan against the cash you need to retain. With assets approaching or exceeding $2 million, request an individual estate-tax analysis that includes the home and all other includible property. If an heir has disabilities or needs financial oversight, address that beneficiary’s trust separately.

These are discussion priorities, not a recommendation to transfer any particular asset.

Living alone: make the backup plan explicit. Choose someone who can check in, respond to an emergency, and help coordinate care. Separately name the people with legal authority to manage finances and health-care decisions. Give the appropriate people a secure way to locate key documents and arrange access to the home when needed.

Bring to the attorney meeting

  • Current deed, mortgage balance, homestead declaration, estimated home value, purchase cost, and major improvements.
  • Account balances, retirement accounts, insurance policies, income, spending, and any long-term-care coverage.
  • Existing wills, trusts, powers of attorney, health-care proxy, and account beneficiary designations.
  • Intended heirs, a trusted emergency contact, health and anticipated care needs, and gifts or transfers during the past five years. If previously married, bring relevant divorce documents or a deceased spouse’s estate and trust records.

Ask for clear answers to these questions

  1. Which specific problem does each proposed trust solve?
  2. Can I change beneficiaries, replace the trustee, sell the house, or buy another home?
  3. Exactly what money can I access if I need care next year?
  4. How do the plan and deed affect MassHealth eligibility, recovery, and homestead protection?
  5. What happens to capital-gains basis and Massachusetts estate tax at my death, and which assets will fund the tax payment?
  6. Who handles the deed, account funding, tax returns, and future administration—and what are the fees?
  7. What happens if the trustee dies, becomes ill, or disagrees with family members?
  8. Who will manage finances and health-care decisions if I cannot, and who is the backup?

Choose a reliable successor trustee, financial agent, health-care agent, and backups; they need not all be the same person. A trust does not replace a durable financial power of attorney, health-care proxy, or a will addressing assets outside the trust. Retirement accounts need separate beneficiary planning; do not simply retitle an IRA into a living trust.

The most useful starting point: decide how much control and access the homeowner needs to retain, then evaluate the trust design. Protecting an inheritance should be weighed against the homeowner’s ability to pay for and choose care.
